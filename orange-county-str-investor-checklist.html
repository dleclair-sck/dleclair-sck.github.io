Before you buy an Airbnb in Orange County: 7 things every STR investor should check.

The most expensive STR mistake can happen before you ever close: buying a property that cannot legally—or profitably—operate the way you intended.

SoCal STR Experts · October 7, 2026 · Investor insight

The address matters more than the listing

Two homes can look nearly identical, sit a few minutes apart, and have completely different short-term rental prospects. In Orange County, STR rules are not one-size-fits-all. The county's unincorporated areas have their own rules, while each incorporated city can set its own requirements.

For example, Irvine prohibits short-term rentals in residential zones, while the City of Orange operates a permit program with a capped number of registrations—and its current program has reached the 125-permit limit. Before you underwrite the revenue, confirm that the property can legally generate it.

1. Confirm the city, zoning and STR rules

What to verify

Identify the exact jurisdiction, zoning designation, whether STRs are permitted, whether new permits are available, and whether the property has any parcel-specific restrictions.

Do not rely on an Airbnb listing—or even on what a neighboring property is doing—as proof that a property is eligible. In Irvine, for example, the city states that operating a prohibited STR and advertising one can violate the zoning ordinance. citeturn0search0

For unincorporated Orange County, the adopted zoning code allows STRs in specified residential and commercial contexts subject to a Short-Term Rental Permit and operating standards. citeturn0search18

2. Check permit availability before you make an offer

A city may technically allow STRs while still limiting how many permits can be active. That distinction matters enormously to an investor.

The City of Orange is a good example: it allows STRs through a permit program, but the city's published materials currently state that the maximum of 125 registrations has been reached and applications are closed except for the waitlist. Permits are also non-transferable. citeturn0search4

In other words, “STRs are allowed here” is not enough. The real question is: Can this specific property obtain and maintain the required authorization?

3. Read the HOA and CC&Rs

City approval does not automatically override private restrictions. If the property is subject to an HOA, review the governing documents and confirm whether short-term rentals, transient occupancy, minimum lease terms, or specific guest-use rules are restricted.

This step belongs in the acquisition process—not after closing. A beautiful property with great projected revenue is not a great STR investment if the governing documents prevent the intended use.

4. Underwrite parking, occupancy and operating restrictions

Permits often come with operating standards that affect the economics of a property. Parking, guest counts, quiet hours, local-contact requirements, and other rules can influence both guest experience and revenue potential.

Unincorporated Orange County's current standards, for example, include minimum off-street parking requirements, vehicle limits, overnight occupancy limits, and quiet-hour noise standards. citeturn0search18

That means bedroom count alone is not a revenue model. The property needs to work within the rules that govern its actual operation.

5. Build the real startup budget

The purchase price is only the beginning. A serious STR acquisition budget should account for renovation, furniture, décor, appliances, linens, technology, photography, exterior improvements, permits, professional services, launch costs, and an appropriate operating reserve.

This is where design and investment strategy should meet. The goal is not to spend the most. It is to identify the improvements that matter most to the target guest while protecting the owner's capital.

6. Stress-test the revenue assumptions

Projected revenue should be based on comparable properties, realistic occupancy, seasonality, nightly-rate positioning, guest capacity, amenities, and the property's actual regulatory constraints—not on the most optimistic number you can find online.

Run multiple cases. What happens if revenue is lower than expected? What if the first year includes additional repairs? What if a permit restriction limits occupancy? What if an amenity requires more maintenance than planned?

The investment should still make sense when the assumptions are tested—not just when everything goes right.

7. Look at the after-tax picture, too

Once the property passes the legal and operating tests, investors can evaluate the broader financial picture, including depreciation and potential cost-segregation strategies.

A qualified cost-segregation study may identify eligible components that receive shorter depreciation recovery periods. Depending on the property and the investor's individual tax circumstances, that can potentially accelerate deductions. It should be evaluated with a qualified tax professional rather than treated as a substitute for sound underwriting.

The biggest mistake is buying first and researching later

An STR should be evaluated differently from a conventional rental or primary residence. The property has to work as a real estate asset, but it also has to work as a regulated hospitality business.

That means the diligence starts before the offer: legal use, permit availability, HOA restrictions, operating limits, startup capital, realistic revenue, and the overall investment thesis.

When those pieces line up, then you can move into the fun part—creating a property guests remember.
